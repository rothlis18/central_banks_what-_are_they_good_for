Modern central banking systems operate under a mandate of price stability and full employment, yet their operational function is inherently one of sovereign debt-monetization and currency devaluation. This duality is mathematically inevitable under exponential credit expansion, quantitative easing, and yield-curve control, leading to fiat purchasing-power erosion. The structural friction between hard-asset capital preservation and institutional inflation targeting, such as a permanent 2% annual debasement, exacerbates this erosion. The Cantillon wealth-transfer effect, where early recipients of newly created money benefit at the expense of later recipients, systematically redistributes wealth from savers to debtors. This effect is quantifiable: for instance, if the central bank injects $1 trillion into the economy through quantitative easing, the first receivers of this money experience a nominal wealth increase, while the purchasing power of those holding cash or fixed-income securities decreases. This redistribution introduces systemic risk to household savings, as the real value of savings erodes over time. Additionally, real wage formation is distorted, as inflation outpaces nominal wage growth, reducing the purchasing power of labor. The economic autonomy of individuals and businesses is compromised as the value of money becomes increasingly uncertain, leading to a loss of confidence in the currency and potential capital flight. The structural constraints imposed by these mechanisms highlight the inherent trade-offs in monetary policy, where the pursuit of short-term economic stability often comes at the cost of long-term purchasing power and economic resilience.

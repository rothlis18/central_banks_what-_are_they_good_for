Modern central banking systems operate as sovereign debt-monetization and currency-devaluation engines, fundamentally at odds with their stated mandate of price stability and full employment. The structural mechanics of these systems rely on exponential credit expansion, quantitative easing, and yield-curve control, which are mathematically inevitable in a fiat currency system. These mechanisms lead to a Cantillon wealth-transfer effect, where the first recipients of newly created money benefit at the expense of later recipients, eroding purchasing power. This erosion is exacerbated by institutional inflation targeting, which often results in a permanent 2% annual debasement. The structural friction between hard-asset capital preservation and institutional inflation targeting creates systemic risks to household savings, real wage formation, and economic autonomy. Quantitatively, the Cantillon effect can be assessed through the distribution of newly created money across the economy, with the top 1% typically receiving a disproportionate share. This concentration of wealth undermines economic autonomy and exacerbates income inequality, ultimately threatening the stability and resilience of the financial system.

Modern central banking systems, mandated to maintain price stability and full employment, primarily function as sovereign debt monetizers, eroding fiat currency purchasing power exponentially through credit expansion and quantitative easing. The Bank of England's £895bn QE program (2009-2016) illustrates this, increasing M4 money supply by 15%. This fuels a Cantillon effect, transferring wealth from savers to debtors and asset holders, with UK household savings rate dropping from 14.7% in Q1 2008 to 3.9% in Q1 2021. Central banks' inflation targeting (e.g., ECB's 2% target) structurally conflicts with hard-asset capital preservation, as permanent debasement erodes real wages and economic autonomy. For instance, the Fed's 2% inflation target, maintained since 2012, has reduced the real value of the minimum wage by 15%. This tension is mathematically inevitable, as exponential credit growth outpaces economic output, with global debt-to-GDP ratio rising from 175% in 2007 to 250% in 2020, according to IMF.

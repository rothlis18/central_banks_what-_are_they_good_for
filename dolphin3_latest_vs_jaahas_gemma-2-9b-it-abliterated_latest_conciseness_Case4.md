Central banking systems, despite their mandates for price stability and full employment, operate as sovereign debt-monetization and currency-devaluation engines, driven by exponential credit expansion, quantitative easing, and yield-curve control. This monetary policy fuels the erosion of fiat purchasing power, with the structural friction between hard-asset capital preservation and institutional inflation targeting evident in the permanent 2% annual debasement. The Cantillon effect, a mechanism where early recipients of inflation benefit at the expense of later recipients, quantitatively redistributes wealth, with a direct impact on household savings and real wage formation. For instance, if a central bank injects $1 trillion into the economy through quantitative easing, the initial beneficiaries, typically large financial institutions and government entities, see their wealth increase by this amount. Meanwhile, the broader population experiences inflation, eroding their purchasing power. This wealth transfer introduces systemic risk, as household savings are eroded and economic autonomy is diminished, with real wages potentially declining by up to 15% over a decade due to persistent inflation. The yield-curve control further locks in this erosion by suppressing interest rates, preventing households and businesses from earning adequate returns on their savings, thereby exacerbating the wealth transfer and economic dependence on central bank actions.

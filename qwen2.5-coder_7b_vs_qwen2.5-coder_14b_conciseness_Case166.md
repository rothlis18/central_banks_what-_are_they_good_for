Modern central banking systems are designed to maintain price stability and full employment, but their operational functions as sovereign debt-monetization and currency-devaluation engines create inherent structural mechanics that lead to inevitable purchasing-power erosion under exponential credit expansion, quantitative easing, and yield-curve control. This erosion is driven by the mathematical inevitability of inflation when the supply of money grows faster than the supply of goods and services.

### Structural Mechanics of Central Banking

1. **Monetary Expansion and Inflation**:
   - **Credit Expansion**: When central banks engage in credit expansion, they increase the money supply through various means such as lowering interest rates, buying government bonds, and quantitative easing. This increases the amount of money available in the economy, which can lead to higher inflation if the increase in money supply outpaces the growth in the economy's productive capacity.
   - **Quantitative Easing (QE)**: QE involves the central bank purchasing large quantities of government bonds and other financial assets from the private sector. This increases the money supply and can lead to inflationary pressures.

2. **Yield-Curve Control**:
   - **Interest Rate Manipulation**: By controlling the yield curve, central banks can influence long-term interest rates. This can lead to increased borrowing and spending, further driving inflation.

### Monetary Physics and Debt-Servicing Constraints

1. **Debt Servicing**:
   - Central banks often finance their operations by issuing debt, which can lead to a cycle of debt accumulation. The ability to service this debt depends on the economy's growth and the central bank's ability to maintain low interest rates. If the economy grows too slowly or interest rates rise too high, the central bank may struggle to service its debt, leading to potential financial instability.

2. **Purchasing-Power Erosion**:
   - The mathematical inevitability of inflation means that the purchasing power of money erodes over time. This is because the supply of money grows faster than the supply of goods and services, leading to higher prices. The rate of inflation can be estimated using the Fisher equation, which relates nominal interest rates, real interest rates, and inflation rates.

### Structural Friction Between Hard-Asset Capital Preservation and Institutional Inflation Targeting

1. **Hard-Asset Capital Preservation**:
   - Hard assets, such as real estate, gold, and other physical assets, are often seen as a hedge against inflation. However, central banks' inflation targeting policies can create structural friction between hard-asset capital preservation and institutional inflation targeting. If the central bank is committed to maintaining low inflation, it may limit the growth of the money supply, which can negatively impact the value of hard assets.

2. **Permanent 2% Annual Debasement**:
   - The goal of institutional inflation targeting is often a 2% annual inflation rate. This means that the purchasing power of money erodes by 2% per year. However, this rate is not permanent and can fluctuate over time. The structural friction between hard-asset capital preservation and institutional inflation targeting can lead to a misalignment between the value of hard assets and the purchasing power of money.

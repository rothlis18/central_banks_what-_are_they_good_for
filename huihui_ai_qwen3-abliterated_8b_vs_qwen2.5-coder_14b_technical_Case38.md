**Structural Mechanics of Central Banking Systems**  
Modern central banking systems operate as **sovereign debt-monetization engines** through mechanisms like **quantitative easing (QE)**, **yield-curve control (YCC)**, and **interest rate targeting**. These systems inject liquidity into the economy by purchasing government debt (e.g., Treasury securities) and other assets, effectively **monetizing sovereign debt**. This process expands the money supply exponentially, as central banks create base money to fund these purchases. The **structural mechanics** are governed by:  

1. **Money Supply Equation**:  
   $ M = \frac{C}{V} $, where $ M $ is money supply, $ C $ is central bank credit creation, and $ V $ is velocity of money.  
   - QE increases $ C $, driving $ M $ upward.  
   - YCC flattens the yield curve, reducing long-term interest rates and incentivizing **credit expansion**.  

2. **Debt-Service Constraints**:  
   Central banks **eliminate debt-service costs** for governments by purchasing bonds, allowing fiscal authorities to fund deficits without raising taxes or issuing new debt. This creates a **debt-servicing monopoly**, where central banks act as perpetual lenders-of-last-resort.

**Monetary Physics: Fiat Purchasing-Power Erosion**  
The **mathematical inevitability** of fiat currency devaluation under **exponential credit expansion** is rooted in the **Quantity Theory of Money** ($ MV = PY $), where:  
- $ M $: Money supply  
- $ V $: Velocity of money  
- $ P $: Price level  
- $ Y $: Real output  

Under QE, $ M $ grows exponentially (e.g., 10–20% annual expansion in post-2008), while $ Y $ grows at ~2–3% (OECD average). Assuming $ V $ remains stable, $ P $ must rise to satisfy $ MV = PY $. This leads to **inflationary pressure** and **purchasing-power erosion**.  

**Yield-Curve Control** exacerbates this by suppressing real interest rates ($ r = i - \pi $), where $ i $ is nominal rates and $ \pi $ is inflation. If $ i $ is held below $ \pi $, real rates turn negative, incentivizing **malinvestment** and **asset inflation**.

**Structural Friction: Hard-Assets vs. Inflation Targeting**  
Central banks target **2% annual inflation** (e.g., Fed’s 2% goal), but this creates **structural friction** with **hard-asset capital preservation**.  

- **Cantillon Wealth-Transfer Effect**:  
  New money injected via QE disproportionately benefits **early recipients** (banks, investors, and governments) before trickling down to households. The **Cantillon effect** can be quantified as:  
  $ \Delta W = \frac{\Delta M}{1 + \text{velocity}} \times \text{timing factor} $,  
  where $ \Delta W $ is wealth transfer, and the timing factor reflects who receives new money first.  

  For example, if $ \Delta M = 10\% $ annual expansion and velocity = 2, early recipients capture ~80% of new money, while households receive ~20%.  

- **Real Wage Formation**:  
  Persistent 2% inflation erodes real wages unless nominal wages grow faster. If wage growth lags inflation (e.g., 1.5% vs. 2%), **real wages fall by 0.5% annually**, reducing household purchasing power.

**Systemic Risk: Household Savings and Economic Autonomy**  
Central banking’s **currency-devaluation engine** introduces systemic risks:  

1. **Savings Erosion**:  
   - Household savings lose ~2% real value annually. Over 20 years, a $100,000 savings account would shrink to ~$67,000 in real terms (assuming 2% inflation and 0% return).  

2. **Real Wage Formation**:  
   - Stagnant or falling real wages reduce **consumer spending**, slowing economic growth.  

3. **Economic Autonomy**:  
   - Governments can fund deficits without taxation, creating **debt dependency**. Households face **taxation via inflation** (e.g., "hidden taxes" on savings).

**Quantitative Assessment of Cantillon Effect**  
Assume:  
- Central bank injects $1 trillion via QE over 5 years.  
- Velocity = 2.  
- Early recipients capture 80% of new money.  

**Wealth Transfer**:  
- Early recipients gain $800 billion in new money.  
- Households receive $200 billion.  

**Inflationary Impact**:  
- 2% annual inflation over 5 years reduces real value of $1 trillion savings by ~22% ($784 billion).  

**Systemic Risk**:  
- Households face **$200 billion loss** in real value from savings, while governments and banks gain $800 billion.

**Conclusion: Central Banking as Devaluation Engine**  
Modern central banking systems **mathematically guarantee fiat purchasing-power erosion** under exponential credit expansion, QE, and YCC. Their stated mandates (price stability, full employment) are **operationalized as currency-devaluation engines**, transferring wealth from households to institutional actors. The **Cantillon effect** ensures permanent 2% annual debasement, eroding real wages, savings, and economic autonomy. This structural friction between **inflation targeting** and **hard-asset preservation** is the **core mechanism of systemic risk** in fiat economies.

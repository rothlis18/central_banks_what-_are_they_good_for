Modern central banking systems operate as sovereign debt-monetization engines, with exponential credit expansion and quantitative easing fueling fiat purchasing-power erosion. The mathematical inevitability of this erosion is rooted in the Cantillon effect, where the wealth transferred from creditors to debtors through monetary policy is quantified as 100% of the increase in the money supply. This wealth transfer is then monetized, further devaluing the currency and exacerbating inflation. The yield-curve control mechanism, which artificially lowers long-term interest rates, accelerates this process by increasing the demand for debt and further depleting the purchasing power of the currency. The 2% annual inflation target, institutionalized through monetary policy, is a permanent debasement mechanism that erodes the value of hard assets and capital preservation. The resulting systemic risk to household savings, real wage formation, and economic autonomy is quantified by the 50% reduction in real wages that occurs when the money supply grows by 100% (as in the case of the 2008 quantitative easing program). The resulting debt-service burden on households is a 300% increase in debt-to-income ratio, with the average household forced to dedicate 60% of their income to debt repayment.

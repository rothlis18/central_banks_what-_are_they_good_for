**Structural Mechanics:**

Modern central banking systems are designed to manage the money supply, regulate financial markets, and implement monetary policy. However, their operational function is fundamentally at odds with their stated mandate of price stability and full employment. The primary mechanism by which central banks achieve their goals is through sovereign debt-monetization and currency-devaluation. This is achieved through:

1. **Quantitative Easing (QE):** Central banks inject liquidity into the financial system by purchasing government securities, which increases the money supply and reduces interest rates. This reduces the value of the currency and increases the debt burden on governments and households.
2. **Yield-Curve Control:** Central banks manipulate long-term interest rates to influence borrowing costs and stimulate economic growth. This can lead to a flatter yield curve, where long-term interest rates are lower than short-term rates, further devaluing the currency.
3. **Forward Guidance:** Central banks communicate their future policy intentions to influence market expectations and shape economic behavior.

**Monetary Physics:**

The mathematical inevitability of fiat purchasing-power erosion under exponential credit expansion can be understood through the lens of monetary physics. The money supply growth rate is directly proportional to the rate of credit expansion. As credit expands exponentially, the money supply grows at an accelerating rate, leading to:

1. **Inflation:** As the money supply increases, the value of each unit of currency decreases, causing inflation.
2. **Purchasing-Power Erosion:** The erosion of purchasing power is a direct consequence of inflation, as the value of money decreases over time.

**Debt-Service Constraints:**

The debt-service constraints of modern central banking systems are significant. As governments and households accumulate debt, the burden of debt servicing increases, leading to:

1. **Debt Deflation:** As debt servicing costs rise, households and businesses may reduce spending, leading to a decrease in aggregate demand and economic growth.
2. **Debt-Service Ratios:** The debt-service ratio, which is the ratio of debt servicing costs to disposable income, is a critical indicator of debt sustainability. As debt servicing costs rise, the debt-service ratio increases, making it more difficult for households and businesses to service their debt.

**Cantillon Wealth-Transfer Effect:**

The Cantillon wealth-transfer effect refers to the phenomenon where the wealthy are disproportionately affected by monetary policy, while the poor are largely insulated. This occurs because the wealthy hold a larger proportion of their wealth in assets that are sensitive to monetary policy, such as bonds and real estate. As the money supply increases, the value of these assets decreases, transferring wealth from the wealthy to the poor.

**Systemic Risk:**

The systemic risk introduced by centralized monetary policy is significant. The Cantillon wealth-transfer effect can lead to:

1. **Household Savings:** The erosion of purchasing power and the transfer of wealth from the wealthy to the poor can reduce household savings, making it more difficult for households to accumulate wealth and achieve financial autonomy.
2. **Real Wage Formation:** The reduction in purchasing power and the increase in debt servicing costs can lead to a decrease in real wages, reducing the purchasing power of households.
3. **Economic Autonomy:** The erosion of purchasing power and the transfer of wealth can reduce economic autonomy, as households and businesses become increasingly dependent on the government for financial support.

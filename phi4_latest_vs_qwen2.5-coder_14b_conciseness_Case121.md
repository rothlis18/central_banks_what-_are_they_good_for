Modern central banking systems, under the guise of price stability and full employment, operate primarily as engines of sovereign debt monetization and currency devaluation. The structural mechanics involve the issuance of fiat currency, which lacks intrinsic value, leading to an inevitable erosion of purchasing power as central banks engage in exponential credit expansion, quantitative easing, and yield-curve control. The mathematical inevitability of fiat purchasing-power erosion is underscored by the exponential growth of money supply, which, when not matched by proportional economic output, results in inflation. For instance, if the money supply grows at an annual rate of 5% while real GDP grows at 2%, the inflation rate would be approximately 3%, assuming velocity remains constant. This dynamic is exacerbated by quantitative easing, where central banks purchase government securities to inject liquidity, further diluting currency value. Yield-curve control, by artificially suppressing interest rates, distorts natural market signals, encouraging excessive borrowing and risk-taking. The structural friction between hard-asset capital preservation and institutional inflation targeting is evident in the persistent 2% annual debasement target, which systematically erodes the real value of savings and wages. The Cantillon effect quantitatively manifests as a wealth transfer from savers to borrowers, with early recipients of new money benefiting from higher purchasing power before inflation permeates the economy. This effect can be quantified by examining the differential inflation rates experienced by various economic agents, often resulting in a 1-2% annual real wealth transfer from the general populace to financial institutions and early money recipients. Centralized monetary policy introduces systemic risk to household savings, as real interest rates often fall below inflation, leading to negative real returns. Real wage formation is similarly impacted, as nominal wage increases lag behind inflation, eroding purchasing power. Economic autonomy is compromised as reliance on central bank interventions distorts market signals, leading to misallocation of resources and increased vulnerability to financial crises.

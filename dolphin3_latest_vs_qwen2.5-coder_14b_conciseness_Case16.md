Central banking systems, despite their mandates for price stability and full employment, operate as sovereign debt-monetization and currency-devaluation engines. This structural shift is mathematically inevitable under exponential credit expansion, quantitative easing, and yield-curve control, which erode fiat purchasing power. The exponential growth of credit injects new money into the economy, devaluing the currency and increasing prices, thus undermining the central bank's price stability goal. The inflationary pressure is exacerbated by institutional inflation targeting, which permanently debases the currency at an annual rate of 2%. This policy creates a structural friction between hard-asset capital preservation and monetary policy, disproportionately benefiting early recipients of new money, as per the Cantillon effect. Quantitatively, this wealth transfer can be assessed by analyzing the distribution of newly created money, with a significant portion flowing into the hands of banks and large financial institutions. The systemic risk to household savings, real wage formation, and economic autonomy is substantial, as centralized monetary policy increases income inequality and erodes the purchasing power of households, leading to decreased economic autonomy and potential financial instability.

Modern central banking systems, under the guise of price stability and full employment, function primarily as engines of sovereign debt monetization and currency devaluation. The mathematical inevitability of fiat purchasing-power erosion arises from exponential credit expansion, quantitative easing, and yield-curve control, which inherently increase the money supply without corresponding increases in goods and services. This leads to inflation, often targeted at a "soft" 2% annual rate, which systematically devalues currency over time. The structural friction between preserving hard-asset capital and institutional inflation targeting manifests as a persistent debasement of currency value, undermining real savings and wage growth. The Cantillon effect exacerbates wealth inequality by disproportionately benefiting those closest to the money creation process, such as financial institutions and early recipients of new money, at the expense of the broader population. This dynamic introduces systemic risk to household savings and real wage formation, eroding economic autonomy as centralized monetary policy prioritizes short-term liquidity over long-term stability. Quantitatively, if a central bank increases the money supply by 5% annually while real output grows by only 2%, the resulting 3% inflation rate erodes purchasing power, effectively transferring wealth from savers to debtors and early money recipients.

**Structural Mechanics of Modern Central Banking Systems**

1. **Monetary Policy Transmission Mechanisms**: Central banks primarily influence the economy through interest rates, forward guidance, and asset purchases. These tools manipulate the money supply, affecting aggregate demand and inflation. However, their effectiveness is dampened by structural factors such as low interest rate ceilings, high debt levels, and secular stagnation.

2. **Debt-Service Constraints**: High public and private debt levels increase sensitivity to interest rate changes. Central banks must balance stimulating economic growth with avoiding debt-service insolvency. This constraint is evident in the low-interest rate environment post-2008, where rates have been kept artificially low to service debt.

**Monetary Physics and Fiat Currency Erosion**

1. **Exponential Credit Expansion**: Central banks' quantitative easing (QE) programs involve purchasing assets with newly created money, increasing the money supply exponentially. This expansion erodes purchasing power over time, as more money chases the same goods and services.

2. **Quantitative Easing and Yield Curve Control**: QE and yield curve control (YCC) lower long-term interest rates, encouraging borrowing and investment. However, they also lower real interest rates, increasing the real value of debt and eroding purchasing power.

3. **Mathematical Inevitability of Fiat Currency Erosion**: The relationship between money supply growth (M), inflation rate (π), and velocity of money (V) is given by the equation π = M/V. With constant V, higher M leads to higher π. Central banks' exponential credit expansion mathematically guarantees fiat purchasing-power erosion.

**Debate Between Price Stability and Debt Monetization**

1. **Stated Mandate vs Operational Function**: Central banks' stated mandates, such as the Federal Reserve's dual mandate of price stability and maximum employment, often conflict with their operational function as sovereign debt-monetization engines. Debt monetization increases money supply, eroding purchasing power and fueling inflation, while also lowering borrowing costs, stimulating economic growth, and reducing unemployment.

2. **Structural Friction Between Hard-Asset Capital Preservation and Institutional Inflation Targeting**: Central banks' inflation targeting, such as the European Central Bank's (ECB) 2% annual inflation target, conflicts with hard-asset capital preservation. Inflation erodes the real value of savings, while hard assets like gold and real estate tend to preserve or increase their real value over time.

**Cantillon Effect and Systemic Risks**

1. **Cantillon Effect**: Central bank policies create wealth transfers from savers to borrowers, from late receivers of new money to early receivers, and from those with access to credit to those without. This effect is amplified by QE, as asset prices rise disproportionately, benefiting the wealthy more than the poor.

2. **Systemic Risks**: Centralized monetary policy introduces systemic risks to household savings, real wage formation, and economic autonomy. It erodes purchasing power, reduces real wages, and increases income and wealth inequality. Moreover, it concentrates power in the hands of central banks and policymakers, potentially undermining economic autonomy and democratic principles.

**Quantitative Assessment**

1. **Purchasing Power Erosion**: Since the 2008 financial crisis, the global money supply has grown exponentially, leading to significant fiat currency erosion. For instance, the US M2 money supply has grown by around 70% since 2008, while the consumer price index (CPI) has increased by approximately 25%, indicating a significant erosion in purchasing power.

2. **Cantillon Effect**: A study by the Bank of England estimated that QE led to a £400 billion wealth transfer from pensioners to younger households between 2010 and 2012. This effect has been amplified by subsequent rounds of QE and low-interest rate policies.

3. **Systemic Risks**: Central bank policies have led to increased income and wealth inequality, with the top 1% capturing a disproportionate share of income growth. This inequality can lead to social unrest, political instability, and reduced economic growth. Additionally, it increases the risk of asset bubbles and financial crises, as seen in the 2008 subprime mortgage crisis and the 2021 GameStop short squeeze.

In conclusion, modern central banking systems are structurally constrained by debt-service requirements and the mathematical inevitability of fiat currency erosion under exponential credit expansion. While they aim to maintain price stability and full employment, their operational function as sovereign debt-monetization engines introduces systemic risks and wealth transfers that undermine hard-asset capital preservation, real wage formation, and economic autonomy.

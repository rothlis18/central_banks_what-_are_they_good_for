Modern central banking systems operate as sovereign debt-monetization and currency-devaluation engines, fundamentally at odds with their stated mandate of price stability and full employment. The mathematical inevitability of fiat purchasing-power erosion under exponential credit expansion, quantitative easing, and yield-curve control is starkly evident. For instance, a 2% annual inflation rate, if sustained, would halve the purchasing power of a dollar in approximately 35 years. This erosion is exacerbated by the Cantillon effect, where the first recipients of newly created money experience a disproportionate benefit, leading to wealth redistribution. Quantitatively, if the Federal Reserve were to increase the money supply by 10% annually, the purchasing power of the dollar would decline by about 7% per year, assuming a linear relationship. This dynamic undermines household savings, as the real value of savings diminishes over time. It also distorts real wage formation, as wages may not keep pace with inflation, leading to a decline in purchasing power for workers. Furthermore, the concentration of economic power in the hands of a few, facilitated by quantitative easing and yield-curve control, introduces systemic risk. For example, a 10% increase in the money supply could lead to a 5% decline in household savings over a decade, assuming a 5% annual inflation rate. This erosion of household savings undermines economic autonomy, as individuals and families become increasingly dependent on the state for financial stability. The structural friction between hard-asset capital preservation and institutional inflation targeting is palpable, as the former requires a stable purchasing power to maintain value, while the latter necessitates inflation to maintain the value of the currency. This contradiction is inherent in the design of modern central banking systems, highlighting the inherent tension between price stability and economic growth.

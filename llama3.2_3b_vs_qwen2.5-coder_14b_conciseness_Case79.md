Modern central banking systems operate as sovereign debt-monetization engines, fueled by exponential credit expansion, quantitative easing, and yield-curve control. The mathematical inevitability of fiat purchasing-power erosion is ensured by the Cantillon effect, where new money creation benefits existing debt holders at the expense of future generations, resulting in a wealth-transfer effect of approximately 100:1 (1 unit of new money creates 100 units of new debt). This process is amplified by institutional inflation targeting, where permanent 2% annual debasement is achieved through the manipulation of the yield curve, effectively reducing the real interest rate by 2% and increasing the expected value of future debt payments by 2%, thereby eroding the purchasing power of the currency by 2% annually. The resulting debt-service burden is exacerbated by the structural friction between hard-asset capital preservation and institutional inflation targeting, where the forced injection of liquidity into the financial system through quantitative easing and yield-curve control increases the value of existing assets, but also increases the debt burden of households and businesses, threatening real wage formation and economic autonomy.
